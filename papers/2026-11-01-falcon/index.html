Efficient Streaming Audio-Visual Target Speaker Extraction for Real-World Acoustic Scenes

Visual mouth cues and multiscale audio processing extract a target speaker, with a training-only interference penalty.

THE TAKEAWAY

Distinguish clip forward-pass cost from streaming buffers and end-to-end delay.

KEEP IN MIND

The reported 51.3 milliseconds is for a two-second clip, not streaming latency. Real-world evaluation includes ten participants and is not a clinical hearing-assistance trial.

READ / COMPLETE NARRATION

The transcript.

Falcon extracts one person's voice from overlapping speech using a microphone mixture and a video of that person's mouth. The visual stream identifies whom to listen to. The output is an estimated speech waveform, not a transcript. This matters for conferencing and potentially hearing assistance, but the paper evaluates speech extraction, not clinical hearing outcomes. Its central question is whether realistic acoustic training and a compact separator can improve quality without excessive computation.

Standard mixtures often contain exactly two speakers. Real scenes contain variable numbers of nearby talkers, distant voices, echoes, ambient noise, and abrupt sounds. RealSSA-Sim combines these factors in about 153 hours of simulated audio-visual mixtures. RealSSA-Real is a separate recorded evaluation set: approximately eighty-four minutes, ten participants, and eight environments. It is never used for training or validation. That is useful distribution-shift evidence, but still a small population and environment sample.

Large time-frequency separators can be expensive. Compact methods such as Swift-Net repeatedly apply a small separation block, trading fewer parameters for repeated computation. Falcon uses an encoder-decoder that reduces resolution, collects coarse scene context, then restores detail at several scales in one pass per block. The audio representation is refined before fusion and again after adding mouth-video features. So single-pass does not mean the whole system contains only one block or has zero buffering.

A frozen visual backbone extracts mouth features. An audio encoder represents the mixture across time and frequency. Recurrent layers and masked attention process those features, while the fused visual cue guides a complex-valued mask that reconstructs target speech. During training, the estimated target's embedding is pulled toward the clean target and pushed away from nearby interfering speakers. Reference embeddings use stopped gradients; the estimated-output path still updates the shared encoder and separator. This extra contrastive objective is combined with waveform reconstruction and removed at inference. It needs separate source waveforms, which simulation supplies but ordinary real recordings do not.

On RealSSA-Sim, scale-invariant signal-to-noise improvement is 7.7 decibels, versus 7.6 for the evaluated causal AV-TF-GridNet baseline. Higher means better separation relative to the mixture. Falcon uses 0.5 million trainable parameters versus 6.2 million, and 10.6 billion multiply-accumulate operations versus 213.8 billion. For a two-second input on an RTX 3090, median forward-pass time is 51.3 milliseconds versus 567.8. Crucially, this is not end-to-end streaming delay: the visual branch buffers 120 milliseconds of future frames, and real streaming scheduling overhead still needs measurement.

The added training loss raises Falcon's separation score from 7.3 to 7.7 decibels without an inference branch. Yet real-recording word error rate remains 32.74 percent and is second-best among the compared extractors. The method is single-channel and depends on visible, aligned mouth information. For always-on multimodal systems, the reusable idea is targeted acoustic supervision and careful separation of model compute from sensor latency. Integration into wearables is plausible, not demonstrated. Paper and project page label NeurIPS 2026; this briefing uses the verified October twenty-third arXiv version, without independently confirming proceedings acceptance.
