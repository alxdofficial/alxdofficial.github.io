Normative Deviation Modeling for EEG-based Depression Detection
First publication: 2026-10-27 | Version: v1 (2026-10-27)
Video produced: 2026-11-01 | Selection digest: 2026-10-30
Publication status: arXiv preprint; peer review not independently verified.
Primary source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37338v1

Can deviations from a learned healthy brain-signal reference help a classifier transfer between datasets? Jiang and colleagues investigate this for major depressive disorder using electroencephalography, or EEG, which records scalp electrical activity. Their models distinguish labeled depression and healthy-control recordings. The important distinction is that this is retrospective classification, not evidence that EEG alone can diagnose depression in routine care.

Brain signals vary across people, electrodes, and recording conditions. A classifier may learn those differences instead of depression-related structure. The study uses two public datasets: Mumtaz2016 has sixty-four people, and MODMA has fifty-three. Signals are mapped to nineteen electrode locations and split into overlapping five-second windows. All windows from a person remain together in within-source cross-validation, preventing simple subject leakage. Thousands of windows still represent only these small cohorts.

The proposed use is a representation aid for future screening research. Conventional models learn directly from diagnostic labels; augmentation methods generate extra training examples. Here, generative models instead define what healthy training data look like. This is a useful change of role, but a reconstruction error can reflect an unfamiliar device or artifact as well as pathology. Healthy-reference modeling is not automatically disease-specific or clinically interpretable.

One reference compresses healthy inputs into a learned dictionary and reconstructs them. A second learns to remove added noise from healthy inputs. Reconstruction and denoising errors then measure deviation from those references. The scalar version combines errors into a score and fuses it with a frozen EEG encoder's features. The feature version retains individual residuals from handcrafted signal features and learns attention-based aggregation across windows. Reference models remain frozen while the downstream fusion and classifier learn depression labels. These are the scalar and feature-level normative-deviation methods.

For Mumtaz-to-MODMA transfer, the scalar method reports seventy-eight point five percent accuracy without target calibration. In reverse transfer, the feature method reports eighty-nine point one five percent, but uses seven known healthy target record groups for score calibration. Those groups are excluded from diagnostic testing. The two numbers are not a controlled head-to-head comparison: direction, aggregation, and target access differ. The feature method's reverse-transfer accuracy exceeds the strongest reported baseline by five point four three percentage points.

The reusable idea for sensing is to supplement an embedding with explicit residuals against a reference distribution. Before adopting it, test whether those residuals detect acquisition shift rather than the phenomenon of interest. Reverse-transfer runs reuse overlapping test groups, so repeated runs are not independent cohorts. Only two small datasets are studied, with no prospective clinical utility or broader population validation. The conference template is not acceptance evidence; treat this as an October twenty-seventh preprint, not a validated diagnostic tool.
