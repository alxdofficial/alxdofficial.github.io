Understanding Everyday Communication Patterns, Stress, and Productivity in Hybrid Work through Egocentric Wearable Audio Sensing
First publication: 2026-10-27 | Version: v1 (2026-10-27)
Video produced: 2026-11-01 | Selection digest: 2026-10-30
Publication status: arXiv preprint; peer review not independently verified.
Primary source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37270v1

Can an always-on sensor reveal how office and home communication relate to stress without retaining raw speech? Feng and colleagues study this with a wearable audio badge. Their target is the wearer's speaking time and vocal activeness, not transcription or clinical diagnosis. The difficult part is separating the wearer from nearby voices using deliberately impoverished acoustic features.

The badge samples twenty seconds each minute and immediately extracts pitch, loudness, and twelve coefficients describing the sound spectrum. Keeping these features instead of recordings reduces the information retained, but does not establish formal privacy. The system combines on-device feature extraction with offline speaker analysis; it is not an entirely on-device intelligent agent. Sparse sampling also means communication is estimated rather than continuously observed.

The practical application is longitudinal workplace research. The authors recruited nineteen employees and analyzed sixteen who wore the badge for more than ten days. Each person participated for four months, averaging about forty recorded workdays. Because private workplace speech could not supply labeled training recordings, the speaker detector was trained on three public conversation datasets. This creates a transfer problem: those datasets use smart glasses or meeting microphones, not the deployed badge.

A large speech model first learns to classify each frame as wearer speech, other speech, or silence. Its predictions then supervise a smaller residual convolutional network that receives only the retained spectral coefficients. The large teacher is frozen; the student is updated to match both the labels and the teacher's probability distribution. This is knowledge distillation. At deployment only the student is used offline. Detected wearer frames yield speaking minutes per hour; pitch and loudness relative to each person's distribution yield activeness.

Diarization error measures incorrect speech timing or speaker assignment. On the three public datasets, distillation reduces that error from sixteen to fourteen point one percent, twenty-four point six to twenty-three point five, and thirty-one point eight to twenty-nine. These are modest, consistent improvements, not near-perfect sensing. In the deployment, higher office speaking load is associated with higher self-reported stress and lower productivity after modeled covariates. Those associations do not show that speaking causes either outcome.

For wearable sensing, the reusable idea is separating a rich training-time teacher from a restricted sensing pipeline. But the deployment is a small cohort from one institution, with no external replication. Public training splits include session-level separation, which does not by itself guarantee unseen speakers. Personalized activeness uses a participant's empirical distribution rather than a proven prospective calibration rule. Mixed-effects models account for repeated observations, but neither many surveys nor months of data turn sixteen people into a large independent sample.
