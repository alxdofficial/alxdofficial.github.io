BeJEPA: A Goal-Conditioned Humanoid Behavior World Model via Unsupervised RL

1. Problem
BeJEPA chooses a humanoid's action using two predictions: what this action changes next, and what the goal-conditioned policy is expected to achieve afterward. That second prediction summarizes future state features, avoiding a long imagined rollout during policy learning. Why is that useful? A robot can copy a pose while standing in the wrong place. Returning may require a step that temporarily worsens posture matching. The controller must value where that step leads.

2. Applications
The physical Unitree G1 demonstrations show returning after a kick, standing from the ground, and changing goals while retaining a world reference. LiDAR supplies position and heading estimates. These are qualitative demonstrations, not a measured hardware success rate. Simulation evaluates twenty-second motion tracking and fixed goals. Manipulation, wearable sensing, and lifelong adaptation are not demonstrated. Those would require new interfaces and experiments.

3. Current state of the art
The relevant baselines are world-input BFM-Zero, SONIC, and Humanoid-GPT. Their original training scales and objectives differ, so the meaningful numerical comparison is this paper's matched evaluation. BeJEPA improves global alignment, but SONIC still wins AMASS local posture. The closest representation precedent is TD-JEPA, which already learns long-term latent predictions. The new question is whether separating immediate physics from behavioral continuation improves humanoid control.

4. Method
A shared encoder maps posture, position, and heading into 256 dimensions. Dynamic predicts the next latent from the current latent and action. Successor predicts discounted future latents under the goal-conditioned policy. Comparing its output with the goal supplies an action value; gradients through both predictors improve the actor. Dynamic learns from observed next states. Successor uses temporal-difference bootstrapping. Both train the representation, emphasizing physical prediction early. Motion priors and physical penalties also guide behavior. A history encoder consumes deployable observations and supplies a latent. At inference, optional model-predictive control corrects the actor using frozen dynamics. Balanced refinement looks sixteen steps ahead and optimizes eight action residuals, then replans. It activates only on slower reference segments.

5. Results
On AMASS, balanced BeJEPA-MPC reaches 219.5 millimetres of global joint-position error, versus 573.4 for world-input BFM-Zero and 531.5 for SONIC: reductions of 61.7 and 58.7 percent. Local posture favors SONIC: 49.7 versus 63.9 millimetres. In the fixed-goal ablation, full BeJEPA's mean position error is 0.642 metres, versus 1.348 for a comparable-capacity merged predictor. Blocking either predictor's representation gradient also hurts. Balanced refinement reduces AMASS tracking lag from 94.8 to 21.2 milliseconds. The tracking tables omit confidence intervals, seed counts, and a precise test-segment list.

6. Novelty and relevance
The contribution is joint learning of local physical prediction and goal-conditioned continuation. For world models, that is a useful design pattern. But uncorrected dynamics drift at longer horizons. Actor plus history encoder total 33 million parameters; all online training networks total 394 million. Training GPU-hours and measured MPC latency are unreported. For continual memory or wearables, relevance is indirect: no episodic recall, no deployment weight learning, and roughly a one-second discount scale. The official demo is live; code is promised before mid-November.
