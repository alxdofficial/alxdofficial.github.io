ECG-SDI: Single-lead Electrocardiogram Enables Continuous Sleep Depth Index Profiling and Scalable Digital Biomarkers for Sleep Health

1. Problem and assumptions
ECG-SDI asks whether one electrical heart-signal lead, or ECG, can produce a continuously varying sleep-depth score, rather than only five stage labels. Its central move is ordinal learning: teach the score that waking is shallower than N1, then N2, then N3. Rapid-eye-movement sleep, or REM, gets a separate classifier because it does not fit that ordering. This assumes cardiac physiology carries useful depth information. The target remains conventional sleep staging, not independently measured continuous depth. This is an October sixteenth preprint.

2. Demonstrated applications
The authors study overnight trajectories, sleep phenotypes and changes around positive-airway-pressure treatment, or CPAP. Their twelve thousand three hundred ninety-two recordings include ten thousand six hundred fifty-two development recordings, seven hundred twenty-six external Cleveland participants, and two nights each from five hundred seven CPAP participants. Recordings are not independent people. Development splits are participant-level. These are retrospective sleep-study cohorts; the wearable demonstration illustrates a workflow, rather than validating home accuracy or treatment decisions.

3. Current research context
Continuous sleep depth is not new. The same group's April 2025 study used EEG, eye movements, muscle activity and ECG with an ordinal objective. March 2026's Holter-to-Sleep supplied an ECG staging backbone. ECG-SDI combines that narrower sensing input with depth ranking and treatment-associated analyses. There is no matched benchmark showing superiority to an EEG-derived continuous-depth index. Its contribution is an accessible sensing direction and cohort analysis, not an established new gold standard.

4. Concrete method
Thirty-second ECG segments are combined with thirty-nine spectral and wavelet features. A convolutional encoder creates each representation. Fifteen-epoch windows feed a Transformer that also sees recording-level context, so this is offline processing. After sleep-stage pretraining, the backbone is frozen. Two residual heads learn REM classification and a scalar depth. Training adds REM cross-entropy to half-weighted pairwise ranking, excluding REM and equal-stage pairs. A crucial dependency follows: the displayed zero-to-one score uses each recording's reference Wake and non-REM labels to choose normalization percentiles. Several reported biomarkers also require reference stages.

5. Results and evidence strength
Raw depth correlates with ordered Wake and non-REM stages at point eight two eight internally and point eight four eight in Cleveland. The CPAP cohort is harder: point four five six before treatment and point five four six afterward. These are stage-agreement results, not continuous-depth accuracy. In the paired analysis, normalized depth area rises six point one percent and shallow-sleep burden falls sixteen percent. Those are relative changes in five hundred seven participants without a randomized control. Cardiac effects of treatment could contribute. The very narrow stage-agreement intervals bootstrap epochs, not independent participants.

6. Novelty, limits and wearable relevance
For wearable research, learning from an inexpensive ECG channel is useful. But the normalized outputs are not yet label-free, and older cohorts dominate development; the CPAP group is mostly men with severe apnea. Mortality associations are observational, not validated individual risk predictions. Home accuracy, power and latency are unmeasured. The public repository has no weights or license, limiting reproducibility. I would treat this as a promising research index whose next test needs label-free calibration, participant-level uncertainty and prospective validation across diverse wearable users.
