Episodic looking - using reinforcement learning to bias perception enhances few-shot and continual learning with protection from forgetting

A reinforcement-learning controller gates a frozen visual representation to study few-shot adaptation and forgetting.

THE TAKEAWAY

Separate stable representations from fast, sparse control.

KEEP IN MIND

Four selected category pairs; repeated inference passes and uneven comparison budgets limit generalization.

1. 1 / Problem

Can a fast-learning controller change how a stable vision model interprets an image, without rewriting that model and forgetting earlier examples? Episodic looking studies this question with a two-part architecture: a frozen image classifier provides reusable visual knowledge, while a small reinforcement-learning controller adjusts intermediate features. It is a feasibility study of complementary learning systems, not a new best-performing continual-learning benchmark result.

2. 2 / Why difficult

A frozen representation protects old knowledge but may fail to separate unfamiliar categories. Updating the whole classifier can learn new examples while damaging old ones. The authors instead let a controller repeatedly reinterpret one image. Unlike moving a camera or choosing a crop, its action changes internal feature strengths. That distinction matters: this is active interpretation of a static image, not demonstrated robotic exploration or event-camera perception.

3. 3 / Applications and prior approaches

All experiments use selected subsets of CIFAR-one-hundred, a dataset of tiny labeled images. A ResNet-eighteen classifier is pretrained on two fine categories within each of twenty broad categories. Four pairs of broad categories then receive three previously unseen fine categories in sequence. Predictions still use the original twenty broad labels. The comparisons include full-classifier fine-tuning and several small classifiers on frozen features. Different seeds, training budgets, and starting accuracies mean these are not equal-compute comparisons.

4. 4 / Method

For each image, the controller runs eight steps. It receives earlier feature summaries, classification scores, and its previous action. Its actor outputs 128 channel multipliers, bounded between zero and two, that gate the frozen classifier after its second residual stage. The classifier then predicts again. During training, reward is the change in probability assigned to the correct broad class. Thus the experimental reward still depends on labels, even though the controller is not given the target label directly. A critic estimates future reward; actor and critic weights update after each step, with clipped policy changes. No gradient updates the image classifier, and there is no replay buffer. A fixed random projection selects just 32 of 1000 hidden units, reducing overlap between updates. At evaluation the learned controller still runs the repeated gating loop without learning from test labels.

5. 5 / Results

Averaged across the four category pairs, final accuracy is 78.0 percent for the proposed controller, 78.4 percent for full-classifier fine-tuning, and 81.3 percent for a sparse frozen-feature classifier. The paper’s forgetting measure is the largest accuracy drop after a category was learned. It falls from 16.0 percentage points with full fine-tuning to 4.6 with the controller; the sparse comparison achieves 4.0. In the aquatic-mammal versus fish few-shot experiment, with sixteen training images per category, accuracy on the newly trained category reaches 78.1 percent, versus 80.5 for full fine-tuning. This advantage does not extend to every aggregate score or to the one-example setting.

6. 6 / Relevance and caveats

The useful mechanism is a sparse, fast controller steering an unchanged intermediate representation. But similar animals still overlap, and more extensively pretrained visual features were harder to steer. The study covers only four chosen category pairs, and repeated passes add computation. Consolidating new experience into the visual model is future work, not implemented here. A sensing system could explore this separation of stable representation and rapid control, but continuous natural streams and real-world deployment remain unverified.
