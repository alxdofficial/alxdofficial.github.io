1 / The problem
A model that both understands and generates images can write a long explanation before drawing, yet extra words do not necessarily improve its picture. This paper asks whether the model should instead carry some of its intermediate computation in continuous hidden states. The proposed system, called LARC, builds on BAGEL, a model with understanding and image-generation components. The key is not to remove language altogether. Short sampled text spans are interleaved with latent steps, so the model retains a trainable stochastic reasoning path while passing richer continuous information to image generation.

2 / Why it matters
The evaluated applications are text-to-image generation, image editing and visual reasoning. A planner that reasons internally before rendering is a plausible broader application, not a deployment shown here. Prior systems often express their intermediate computation entirely as text, which may discard spatial or perceptual information. Other latent-reasoning methods motivate continuous steps, but the important empirical question is whether these steps change the generated image usefully. Longer hidden trajectories alone do not establish better reasoning; they need a training signal and a controlled comparison.

3 / What happens inside
After a special latent marker, the understanding component feeds its final hidden state back as the next input embedding. Several such steps can occur before text generation resumes. The image-generation component reads the accumulated trajectory through attention. Initial supervised training progressively introduces these latent steps. Text prediction and image-denoising objectives train the understanding and generation components while the visual tokenizer stays frozen. There is no ground-truth hidden-state chain. But training still uses paired context and target images: absence of annotated reasoning is not absence of supervised data.

4 / How the reward works
The next stage samples eight alternative trajectories for a context. A frozen reference model tries to predict the target image once with a trajectory and once without it, using the same noise and diffusion time. A trajectory receives a better reward when it reduces the reference model’s image-prediction loss. A separate diversity reward discourages all latent steps from becoming identical. Rewards are normalized within the sampled group to update the understanding component; the image-generation component is no longer updated. This is a loss-based usefulness proxy, not proof of causal reasoning or exact information gain.

5 / Results and cost
On GenEval, a detector-based benchmark of compositional image generation, the author-reported overall score improves from BAGEL’s zero point eight-one to zero point eight-eight. Attribute binding improves from zero point six-three to zero point seven-eight. The ablation reports zero point eight-three after supervised training and zero point eight-eight after the reward stage, so the gain is not only adding latent steps. On one A-one-hundred GPU with batch size one, increasing from one to ten latent steps changes per-sample time from forty-six point eight to forty-eight seconds. Ten separate BAGEL samples take four-hundred-sixty-nine seconds; that is a different sampling procedure, not an equal-compute comparison.

6 / What is reusable
The most reusable idea is to evaluate an internal computation by whether a frozen model predicts the intended output better when given that computation. The diversity ablation matters: without it, the latent steps nearly collapse to copies. These experiments use one underlying model and selected benchmarks, with some model-judged metrics. Broader state-of-the-art status and independent reproduction are unverified. Training reportedly costs three-hundred GPU-hours for supervision and ninety-six for reinforcement learning. The connection to world models is speculative: this paper improves unified image tasks, not persistent memory, continual adaptation or a learned physical simulator.
