Generative Representation Learning for Scalable Latent Tokenization

What makes a good representation for generating images? It may be different from what makes a good representation for classifying them. This paper introduces RePR, or Relative Prediction Representations, and asks us to count the cost of learning the representation as well as the cost of training the generator.

Imagine adding a unique watermark to every training image. A model learning to distinguish images can solve its task by recognizing the watermark. It may have little incentive to preserve the object, pose, lighting, or scene structure. A generator still needs those details. That is the feature-suppression problem the authors study.

Their starting point is pointwise mutual information. In plain language, it measures how much seeing one part of an image helps predict another. Learning by contrasting examples is already related to this idea, but estimates it indirectly by distinguishing positive and negative pairs. The proposed method tries to estimate the predictive advantage directly.

The architecture contains two learned networks. A prediction model receives masked image context and denoises a target patch. It does this with and without additional context. The difference in prediction quality estimates how much information that context contributes. A representation model then learns vectors whose dot products match that information signal.

There are two important practical changes. First, estimating the information in a whole image is expensive. RePR works with incremental patches. It compares the representation before and after adding a patch, and supervises that change with the patch’s conditional information gain. These local increments can add up to the full quantity.

Second, not every predictable bit is equally useful. Very fine texture can dominate an unweighted information objective. The authors weight diffusion noise levels to emphasize the information scales they want. That means the practical method learns a weighted approximation, rather than an exact universal information metric. In fact, one literal PMI-like weighting becomes numerically unstable in their ablation.

The evaluation is unusually useful because it charges both sides of the pipeline. The direct-pixel baseline scores F I D two point three six on guided ImageNet generation. It uses three point five seven times ten to the twentieth floating-point operations. The new representation plus Latent Forcing scores two point one eight. Its total compute is slightly lower: three point four nine times ten to the twentieth operations. That includes learning the representation.

This supports a specific conclusion: spending part of the budget on an appropriate representation can outperform spending everything on the generator. It is a measured improvement at roughly matched compute.

The much larger headline numbers need a different interpretation. Seven times fewer encoder FLOPs than DINOv2, and eight times fewer than WebSSL, are projections from fitted scaling trends. Those equal-quality endpoints were extrapolated. Those projections are not measured speedups for the complete training pipeline. The widest ImageNet model also starts to overfit after two hundred epochs.

For a research lab, the reusable idea is to supervise representation geometry using conditional prediction advantage, then judge it by generative performance under a complete compute budget. The watermark experiment is a useful stress test for shortcuts, although it does not establish that every real-world representation fails this way.

Finally, reproducibility is still incomplete. The linked repository exists, but the inspected version contains only a license. There is no verified runnable implementation or checkpoint today. The paper’s original architecture and scaling figures are available under Creative Commons attribution. They make a good technical walkthrough while implementation catches up.
