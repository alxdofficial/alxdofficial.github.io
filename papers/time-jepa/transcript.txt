Time-JEPA: Grounding Video World Models with Physical Time Series

1 / Problem and central idea
Time-JEPA gives a video model an extra job during training: recover physical measurements at the moment they happened. Those measurements supervise training; evaluation uses video alone. That is the whole mechanism in one sentence. A video can show someone bending a knee, but a useful representation should also preserve how the angle and motion change.

2 / Demonstrated applications
The study covers driving, robot manipulation, and human motion. It tests current physical-state decoding, prediction up to one second ahead, and reconstruction of trajectories or limb distances. Separate tests examine physical anomalies and future object contact. Wearable sensing is a possible extension. The human-motion targets here are pose-derived quantities, not an evaluated wearable inertial-sensor pipeline.

3 / Position among existing methods
The starting point is the released V-JEPA 2.1 video encoder. Physical supervision itself is not new: earlier methods already ground visual representations with physical state. Time-JEPA's distinction is a simple, temporally aligned regression interface across three domains. The strongest comparison is against the same backbone fine-tuned on the same videos without that extra supervision. It is not a universal planning leaderboard result.

4 / Architecture, loss and inference
One branch sees masked video and predicts missing latent features. A slowly updated target encoder supplies the answers. The added branch uses the same online encoder on the full clip. At each temporal position, it averages spatial features and predicts physical quantities through a small head. Measurements are aligned, pooled over adjacent frame pairs, and normalized. Training combines latent prediction with a robust regression loss. After training, discard the auxiliary head, freeze the encoder, and train new task-specific probes, meaning small supervised readout models. Evaluation uses video alone. Variables called out-of-distribution were excluded from encoder supervision, but their probes still receive training labels. This is not zero-shot discovery.

5 / Results and mechanism checks
Against matched video-only fine-tuning, human-motion current-state R-squared, where higher means more variation is explained, rises from 0.231 to 0.345 on seen conditions. For robot manipulation, it rises from 0.424 to 0.725. These are representation-probing results, not task-success rates. With multi-domain training, future-state normalized error under both variable and condition shifts falls from 0.911 to 0.844. Reconstructing human limb distances under held-out conditions reduces mean absolute error from 7.12 to 4.56 centimeters. The crucial ablation changes timing. Correct human-motion alignment gives a correlation of 0.589. A 0.8-second offset gives 0.575. Measurements from another take reduce it to 0.510, barely above the video-only control's 0.503. Local correspondence matters more than simply supplying plausible numbers.

6 / Limits and relevance
There are limits. Physical-anomaly accuracy on GRASP reaches only 57.8 percent, and Physion's all-scenario accuracy is essentially unchanged. Training adds an extra encoder pass, and wearable-device latency is not reported. No continual learning or closed-loop control is demonstrated. For wearable research, the next experiment is synchronized motion supervision, followed by cross-person and device-shift tests and a real downstream decision. That is a proposal, not a demonstrated application. Time-JEPA offers a simple way to make video features more physically informative, with unusually useful synchronization checks.
