A prospective randomized study of patient-centered AI for clinical visit preparation

A randomized same-model study tests structured preparation against a minimally prompted chatbot, with separate clinician and patient outcomes.

Original paper: Figure 4, clinical-utility overall columns · Xincheng Tan et al. (2026) · CC BY 4.0. THE TAKEAWAY

Hold the model fixed and evaluate expert adequacy separately from user confidence.

KEEP IN MIND

Selected English-speaking U.S. app users; incomplete sessions and small visit follow-up limit claims about clinical benefit.

The transcript.

First publication: 2026-10-30 | Version: v1 (2026-10-30) Status: arXiv preprint; peer-reviewed venue not verified. Video produced: 2026-11-03 | Intended digest: 2026-11-03 Primary source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41437v1

1 / Problem Can a chatbot help patients prepare for a medical visit, rather than attempt to replace the clinician? This prospective randomized study tests that narrower goal. The system gathers context, explains what a visit may involve, helps patients prioritize questions, and creates a summary to bring along. The endpoint is preparation quality and patient-reported readiness. It is not diagnostic accuracy, improved treatment or better health outcomes. That distinction matters because a reassuring conversation can feel helpful without making the eventual clinical encounter better.

2 / Why difficult Preparation has several dimensions that a single benchmark misses. The response must be medically appropriate, relevant to this person, comprehensive enough to be useful, and easy to act on. Patient satisfaction and clinician judgments need not agree. The experiment therefore combines independent clinician review with immediate patient surveys and a later follow-up. All arms use the same Gemini two point five Flash language model and the same interface. That helps isolate the contribution of the structured instructions, rather than confounding them with a larger or newer model.

3 / Study and comparator Participants were English-speaking United States adults using the Google Health app on Android. They were randomized one to two to two into a minimally prompted control, a standard structured agent, or a concise structured agent. The main comparison combines the two structured arms. Of five thousand nine hundred sixty randomized users, eight hundred two contributed eight hundred sixty-six eligible health conversations. This is a modified intention-to-treat analysis, not an outcome for everyone randomized. Clinician review covers two hundred seventy-two completed conversations. Immediate surveys cover four hundred sixty conversations, so those denominators must not be interchanged.

4 / Operational solution No model weights were trained or modified. At inference, a system prompt guides the same language model through five steps. First, gather only the context needed for preparation. Second, set expectations about the upcoming visit. Third, explain relevant general health information. Fourth, build a prioritized agenda of questions and topics for the clinician. Finally, update a structured summary after every conversation turn. The workflow follows the patient’s selected goals rather than conducting a full medical history. Even the control maintains a summary, so the comparison is not summary versus no summary. The full prompts are supplied in the paper appendix.

5 / Observed outcomes Among completed sessions reviewed by clinicians, comprehensive preparation was rated positively in sixty-seven point two percent of structured-agent sessions versus fifty point six percent for control. That is sixteen point six percentage points, not a sixteen percent relative gain. Patient alignment improved from ninety point four to ninety-eight point nine percent. Medical safety ratings did not differ significantly; this does not prove absence of rare harms. In immediate surveys, seventy-three point eight percent of structured-agent respondents felt prepared, versus fifty-seven percent for control. These measures use favorable ratings on a five-point scale. Only fifty-nine follow-up respondents had actually attended a visit, and differences in that small group were not statistically significant.

6 / Caveats and reuse The strongest reusable idea is an evaluation design: hold the model fixed, change the workflow, and measure both expert and user perspectives. Repeated sessions were checked in a first-session sensitivity analysis, but clinician ratings exclude unfinished conversations, creating possible survivorship bias. Participants were self-selected, technologically engaged United States app users. Pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections and known mental health disorders were excluded. There was no independent external clinical-site replication or measured patient health benefit. This is promising evidence for a bounded preparation assistant, not a general license for autonomous medical advice.
